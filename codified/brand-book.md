# Agency State brand book


## Agency State Brand Platform

### Agency State Brand Platform, 1. Identity

Agency State helps brand marketing teams and their agencies use AI to produce marketing work they can stand behind. Greg Appler leads the work with his brand marketing judgment, his recommendations, and hands-on building. He helps teams give AI the brand guidance it needs, build automations and agents, and set up checks and human review. He brings more than twenty years of brand marketing leadership and two years of building with Claude Code, and more recently Codex.

Agency State's Brand OS is the approach he brings to that work. It connects one approved brand source AI can read with the automations and agents that source is served to, checks on their work, and the people who review the work and make the judgment calls. It has five parts:

- **Source:** the codified brand.
- **Production:** automations and agents that make the work.
- **Control:** checks against the source and the brand's rules.
- **Feedback:** approved updates to source, checks, and review paths.
- **Operators:** people who make the decisions and keep the system current.

Not every engagement is a full Brand OS build. Greg starts with a brand-readiness assessment and a conversation about where AI fits the team's work, then builds what that shows is worth building, from a first automation or agent to a full Brand OS. He then works closely with the team's Brand OS lead, who looks after the process that keeps the work on brand. His ongoing part is tracking where the AI work misses the brand, making approved updates to the source and checks, and extending the system.

The name reads "agency" as the capacity to act, not agency as a marketing shop.

**Lines:**

- **Tagline.** "On brand, at the speed of AI." With the mark and wordmark, on social cards, decks, and formal contexts. Pair it with the descriptor wherever a reader may not yet know Agency State.
- **Descriptor and positioning line.** "Helping brand marketing teams put AI to work." The plain statement of what Agency State does, and the short form of the homepage introduction. Use it in page and social descriptions, beside the tagline when it needs context, and as a short opening statement of the work.
- **Value lines.** What the reader gets from the work. For Agency State: "marketing work they can stand behind." For Brand OS: it "gives the team a basis for deciding whether its AI work represents its brand and is ready to ship." Example, the homepage introduction: "This gives teams a basis for deciding and helps them stand behind the work they produce." The lines set the meaning; copy can reword them to fit. For how Brand OS works, use the five parts above.

### Agency State Brand Platform, 2. What Agency State delivers

Agency State works with a brand marketing team and its agencies on the brand guidance AI receives, the automations and agents that use it, and the checks and human review around them. Brand OS organizes that work in five parts: Source, Production, Control, Feedback, and Operators. An engagement can cover one part of that work or all of it.

**Source** is the codified brand, structured material a machine can read and a team can govern. Each entry records where it came from and whether it was stated or inferred, and interpretations, conflicts, and gaps go to the team to decide. Each client brand has its own source. Once approved, the relevant parts of the source are served into the tools where the work happens.

**Production** is the automations and agents that make the team's marketing work, such as preparing briefs, updating product pages, or adapting campaign material for different channels. The team supplies the brief and the idea; the codified brand supplies the facts, approved claims, voice, and visual guidance for the task.

**Control** separates what a machine can check from what a person should decide. Automated checks use the same approved source to flag potential errors, and passing a check establishes only what it tested. A person on the team reviews every draft. The reviewer receives the draft with the check results, any limits the checks report, and the questions that need human judgment, such as whether the work emphasizes the right benefits, fits the campaign, and sounds like the brand. Decision owners get the questions the reviewer cannot settle.

**Feedback** records corrections and what was decided. A correction may fix one draft or show a need to update the source, checks, or review process. The person responsible for that decision approves the change before it is applied to future work, so an edit to one draft does not automatically become a rule. Approved updates serve two purposes: improving how the codified brand informs the work, and incorporating changes to the brand itself.

**Operators** are the people who direct the work, review drafts, approve exceptions and updates, and keep the system current. The team keeps authority over its brand decisions. The Brand OS lead looks after the process, working with the people responsible for brand, product, claims, and design decisions.

**The shared working environment.** Where a team needs it, Greg also helps set up how it works in Claude Code and Codex: the project files and repositories, the shared instructions, and how agents receive brand guidance and project instructions. It gives people a consistent way to work together and maintain what they build. Greg builds on the team's existing working environment where it supports the agreed work.

**How Greg works with a team.** He starts with a brand-readiness assessment, looking at the brand as it is written down and as it shows up in the work. The assessment establishes whether the brand is ready to codify, what needs deciding first, and what is worth building, and it is where the team and Greg discuss where AI fits before choosing a build. The brand itself stays with the team and its agencies. He organizes the material with them, drawing on the existing written and visual material, and brings disagreements, interpretations, and gaps to the people who decide them. The team reviews the structured source before it is approved for use by its AI tools.

Once the source is approved and being served, Greg and the team choose and build the first production automations and agents, agree on how every draft is reviewed, and decide how success will be measured. He then works closely with the team, especially its Brand OS lead, tracking where the AI work misses the brand, making approved updates to the source and checks, and extending the system to more of the team's work, such as briefs, production, and reporting.

Agency State takes on projects from a first automation or agent to a full Brand OS.

### Agency State Brand Platform, 5. Voice

### Agency State Brand Platform, Core rules

**The voice rule.** Would this read like one practitioner talking to another about work they both take seriously? If yes, on-brand. If it reads like a vendor pitching or a consultant performing, off-brand.

**Point of view.** Default to first person: I built, I help, I embed. "Agency State" appears where it does structural work (the brand mark, the legal entity, positioning lines). Corporate "we believe" is forbidden. Action-statement "we" is allowed when client and Greg are doing something together.

**Principles, with examples:**

- **Concrete over abstract.** Name the thing, the number, the work.
  - ✓ "Built a twelve-member team."
  - ✗ "Drove transformative growth across the marketing organization."
  - ✓ "Paid media program over $1M a year."
  - ✗ "Significant paid media investment."

- **First person where it fits.** Say what Greg does, found, or thinks as "I," and name the basis.
  - ✓ "I help them give AI the brand guidance it needs, build automations and agents, and set up checks and human review."
  - ✗ "Agency State empowers organizations to harness the power of AI."
  - ✓ "In a test I ran with Yowie, a demo outdoor-gear brand, a product page for a cooler that didn't exist passed every rule check."
  - ✗ "Testing has shown that rule checks alone are not enough."

- **Establish relevance before describing capability.** When introducing the work, start with the reader's task, decision, or the result they need, then say what Greg does. First person doesn't require opening with "I." Check: does the reader know why the work matters before hearing what Greg offers?
  - ✓ "Brand marketing teams and their agencies need to know whether the work they make with AI represents their brand and is ready to ship. I help them give AI the brand guidance it needs, build automations and agents, and set up checks and human review."
  - ✗ "I help brand marketing teams and their agencies use AI in Claude Code and Codex."

- **Give the result its own sentence.** When copy explains both how something works and what the reader gets, separate them. Check: is the result buried at the end of a list of what the system does?
  - ✓ "I help brand marketing teams and their agencies give AI the brand guidance it needs, build automations and agents, and set up checks and human review. This gives teams a basis for deciding and helps them stand behind the work they produce."
  - ✗ "It turns a brand into one source AI can read, serves that source to the team's automations and agents, and helps the team make better calls about which AI work represents its brand and is ready to ship."

- **Close with a next step the reader can take.** End an introduction with something the reader and Greg can start together. A statement of what Greg is available for describes his status and reads like a resume. Check: does the close describe a next step or Greg's status?
  - ✓ "To talk about working together, send me an email."
  - ✗ "I'm available for projects ranging from a first automation or agent to a full Brand OS."

- **Confident without performing.** State a position and move on. No emphasis-by-italics, no exclamation, no transformation language. If a sentence is doing performative work, sounding tidy, clever, or branded, cut it.
  - ✓ "The source is the team's existing brand, written so a machine can read it."
  - ✗ "Our game-changing artifacts will revolutionize your workflow!"
  - ✓ "The work uses Claude Code; other agents work too."
  - ✗ "We leverage best-in-class, cutting-edge AI to unlock your full potential."

- **Honest over polished.** A line that is slightly rough but true beats one that is smooth but performing.
  - ✓ "I'm a generalist with the tools to execute across disciplines, not a paid-media specialist."
  - ✗ "Our full-service team delivers best-in-class results across every channel."

- **Cut what's already implied.** If a clause restates what the words before it already entail, delete it and keep the single strongest version. Same for intent language ("designed to," "built to") where the active verb says it plainly.
  - ✓ "The team reviews each draft."
  - ✗ "The team reviews each draft to make sure every draft gets reviewed."

- **Limit signpost sentences.** A signpost sentence announces the turn before making it: "That changes the role of review," "The distinction matters," "Here is the practical value." Use one only when the reader needs help following the explanation. Prefer letting the next concrete sentence carry the point.
  - ✓ "A person is no longer trying to catch every banned word, every off-palette color, every wrong font, and every missing source by feel."
  - ✗ "That changes the role of review."

- **Plain words over coined terms.** Prefer language the reader already understands. Skip invented labels (for example, "brand-core") when a plain phrase expresses the meaning. Explain the work with its actual objects and actions, without metaphors.

- **Sentence case for titles and labels.** Use sentence case for page titles,
  section labels, cards, notes, buttons, and article titles. Preserve proper
  nouns and fixed terms: Agency State, Brand OS, Claude Code.
  Short 11px monospace labels on the site (row numbers, card meta) may be set
  in all caps as a small-label style (brand guidelines, Typography).
  - ✓ "What is Brand OS?"
  - ✓ "Make the brand usable"
  - ✗ "Make The Brand Usable"

- **No em dashes.** Recast with a comma, parentheses, or two sentences. Do not swap in a colon for the same break. Verbatim quotes keep their source punctuation.

- **Define positively.** The broader rule behind the antithesis ban: say what a thing is, and describe the work by what it produces. Avoid defining by negation or by diminishing a foil ("less like documentation and more like infrastructure," "wiring is the easy part," a catalog of the reader's gaps). Deficiency framing also mis-fits the audience: these are intentional brands, and the gap is form, never their maturity. A contrast survives only when the contrast itself is the point.

- **"X, not Y" antithesis is off by default.** The comma-pivot reversal ("a repo you own, not a tool you rent," "encoded, not carried in memory") is a tic once it recurs. State the point positively instead. Allowed only where the contrast itself is the point and earns its place; if there's no clear cause, cut the "not Y" half.

- **Watch the list tic.** A long inline comma-series, especially five to seven items, starts to read generated. Prefer prose, one concrete example, or a real bulleted list when the items matter, and keep only the items that carry information. This is a tendency to watch, not a hard count: some lists are load-bearing and earn their length.
  - ✓ "It reads the compiled brand and checks copy against the rules the brand has already settled."
  - ✗ "It reads the source, project history, handoffs, drafts, rules, skills, and decisions."

### Agency State Brand Platform, Writing for Agency State

The core rules above, with the §7 never-say list applied strictly.

- **Relaxed, professional explanation is the default.** A practitioner explaining real work to a peer, clearly enough for a reader without an engineering background.
- **The references are the current homepage and the finished site articles, especially What is Brand OS? and Context is not control.** Match their relaxed, specific explanation and the approved reference passages below.
- **Vary sentence length within connected paragraphs.** Short sentences need their surrounding context.
- **Introduce a technical term through the work it does.** Explain technical details far enough for the reader to understand what they establish.
- **Name the thing.** When "it," "this," or "them" could point at more than one thing, repeat the noun.
- **Say what the work does.** Avoid stacking "needs to" and "should" into a list of requirements; describe what happens and who does it.
- **Keep material limits with the claim,** close enough that the reader can't miss them: "none of the checks in this test," not "no check."
- **Personal pieces.** On the homepage and other introductions, Greg's own experience, opinions, and asides are welcome when a real fact or idea sits under them. Cut any that cannot point to what they rest on.
- **Paragraphs connect.** Let paragraphs run a few sentences that build; avoid stacks of one-sentence paragraphs unless a line has earned its isolation.
- **Keep the offer quiet.** Describe the work and what it has shown. No pricing, packages, or hard offers in public copy; an assessment as the first step can be mentioned plainly.
- **An objection is about one passage.** Rejecting a word in one sentence is evidence about that sentence; it does not ban the word everywhere.

**Reference passages.** From Context is not control (agencystate.ai/context-is-not-control.html). Match their voice.

**Terms with fixed meaning:**

- **Artifacts.** The reusable units an engagement produces: prompts, skills, agents, workflows, integrations, documentation. Team-portable.
- **Workflow.** The process itself, what the team actually runs. Active vocabulary for what gets built.
- **Codified brand.** The existing brand expressed as structured source a machine can read and a team can govern: positioning, audiences, voice, vocabulary, claims, products, proof, visual rules, and brand decisions. It is built from the team's written material and approved work, then compiled into the files a model reads and the checks use. Each entry records where it came from and whether it was stated or inferred. Inferences, conflicts, and gaps go to the people who decide them for review. The codified brand is Source, one of the five parts of Brand OS; it does not replace the team's brand development work.
- **Brand OS.** Agency State's approach to putting AI to work on a brand: one approved brand source AI can read, the automations and agents that source is served to, checks on their work, and the people who review the work and make the judgment calls. In five parts: Source (the codified brand), Production (the automations and agents that make the team's work), Control (checks against the source and the brand's rules, with every draft reviewed by a person and questions sent to decision owners), Feedback (approved updates to source, checks, and review paths), and Operators (the people who make the decisions and keep the system current). Use **Agency State's Brand OS** on first reference and **Brand OS** after that. An engagement may build one part or the whole. Agency State builds it with the team, then works closely with its Brand OS lead to track where the AI work misses the brand, make approved updates to the source and checks, and extend the system. Internal tools, repositories, and commands retain their technical names. Public copy uses Brand OS in place of the former BrandBuilder name.
- **Brand OS lead.** The person on the team who looks after the process that keeps its AI work on brand, including adoption, team communications, and keeping the source current. The lead works with the people responsible for brand, product, claims, and design decisions. On a small team, one person may fill several roles.
- **Agents.** In Production, the agents that make the team's work from a brief, with the codified brand supplying the brand side. In Control, review agents read what the team ships and flag what is off-brand before it goes out; a review panel is several review agents reading the same work, each on a different lens. Say "automations and agents" for Production as a whole.
- **Automations.** In Production, the steps that run on their own when something triggers them, with no choices along the way: updating a changed fact everywhere it appears, running the checks, sending each version for review. Agents make the choices within a brief; automations carry the set steps around them. Say "automations and agents" for Production as a whole; use one word alone only when the sentence describes that kind of work.
- **Brand-readiness assessment.** The public name for the first step with a team. It establishes whether the brand is ready to codify, what needs deciding first, and what is worth building, and it is where the team and Greg discuss where AI fits before choosing a build.
- **Provenance.** Whether the codified brand's own source labels are honest: did a rule marked as verbatim actually appear in the source, does a cited section exist. A check on the codified brand and its source labels, not on generated copy. Distinct from a source check.
- **Rule check.** A deterministic check that tests finished copy or markup against rules the brand has already settled: banned words, required claim conditions, approved colors, fonts, product names. It has a right answer and does not wait for a person. Say "rule check" in client copy, not "the linter."
- **Source check.** A check of whether a claim points to something real, for example whether a named product exists in the catalog. It is triage: it flags where to look, it does not declare a flagged item false.
- **Serving layer (MCP).** The client-run server that serves the codified brand into the tools where the work happens, exposing it as context and checks. Say "serving layer" in prose; "a client-run MCP server" when naming the mechanism.
- **Demo brand.** A practice brand built to exercise the system, shaped like a real one, with a brand book, product facts, voice, and visual rules. Name it plainly when it is shown (Yowie, a demo outdoor-gear brand); never imply a demo brand is a client.
- **Recurring work.** The marketing work that comes around regularly (formats, adaptations, channel versions), which Production takes on so the team spends its time on higher-impact work. "Recurring" means it happens regularly, not that it is done identically.

### Agency State Brand Platform, 7. Never say

- **Fear framing.** "don't get left behind," "AI is coming for your job," "your competitors are already doing this." Being plain about what AI automates, as the buyer's leverage, is not fear framing. The ban is on threatening the reader; honesty about the work is fine.
- **Overclaim.** "transform your career," "change your life," "10x your output," "save 20 hours a week," "AI transformation."
- **SaaS clichés.** "unlock," "amplify," "empower," "disrupt," "synergy," "game-changer," "revolutionize," and *leverage* as a verb.
- **Consultant-speak.** "our proprietary methodology," "best-in-class," "thought leadership."
- **Fake proof.** Testimonial-style claims or outcome numbers before the first engagements complete.
- **Defensive copy answering unraised objections.** "no subscription, no vendor lock," "no IT required." If the buyer didn't ask, don't answer. The same goes for telling readers they will own what gets built ("a Brand OS they own"): ownership is implied.
- **Hedge words.** "we believe," "we think," "may," "might." Exception: in founder first person, "I think," "I've found," and "my pick" stake a claim rather than hedge, and are allowed. Corporate "we think" is still forbidden. "May" and "might" are allowed when they describe a specific possibility or an evidence-based limit. Make clear what is uncertain; don't strengthen a claim merely to avoid a hedge word.
- **"Scale yourself."** Treats the human as an asset to be multiplied.
- **AI-first, AI-native, AI-powered.** Buzzword overclaim. Describe what the AI actually does in the work.
- **"AI Enablement."** A retired name for the work. Say what Agency State does instead.
- **Generic "AI strategy" as a deliverable.** Too abstract. Name what is being built.
- **"Capability transfer" or "teaches you to build" as promises.** Overstates what an engagement delivers. Greg builds with the team and provides artifacts and documentation, then works with the Brand OS lead as the system grows. That does not transfer capability in a single handoff.
- **Negative positioning and unmeasured group claims.** No defining Agency State against others ("Unlike enterprise consulting...," "not another AI course") and no characterizing how other brands, teams, or people operate ("most brands," "most teams," "everyone," "nobody"). The defect is asserting a proportion nobody counted, or casting the reader's peers as the ones getting it wrong. A specific, factual comparison is allowed: name the vendor or category, state what each approach does, and keep every claim verifiable; the superiority has to be visible in the facts, not asserted around them. Lead with what Agency State does, and state a failure by what goes wrong rather than by who has it.
- **Mechanical-repetition framing.** "repeatable," "does the same thing every time," "the same way every time." Frames the AI as a deterministic script and undersells its judgment. Describe what the AI actually does, including how it handles novel situations. ("Recurring work" stays fine; "recurring" means happens regularly, not done identically.)
- **Filler and bait.** Hedging filler ("in many ways," "at the end of the day") and CTA-bait closes (a rhetorical "What about you?", "let me know in the comments").
- **Announced honesty, in any form.** "honestly," "to be honest," "I'll be honest," "truthfully," and the adjective versions: "the honest answer," "an honest look," "the honest version," "honesty ledger." The straight-talk voice proves candor by being direct, so announcing it signals the opposite and reads as a filler tic. Say the thing plainly. Describing a property under verification is different and fine when a plainer word does not exist, though "accurate" usually does.
- **Self-undermining maturity hedges.** Repeated "proof so far," "the working parts," "not yet proven," "this is early." Scope once, plainly, then move on; repeating the hedge signals doubt and buries the claim. This does not relax the overclaim or fake-proof bans; it keeps honest scoping from turning into self-doubt.
- **Arguments from the OS metaphor.** "Brand OS" may label, never argue. No claim is true "because it is an operating system," and no copy derives a property from the metaphor (mandatory, total, invisible, "the layer everything runs through"). The test: replace "Brand OS" with "this setup" and every claim must still hold. The name is a handle for the assembled whole; the definition and the evidence carry the weight.

### Agency State Brand Platform, 9. What Agency State is not

*These are internal boundaries for scoping the work, not lines for public copy.*

- **Not a marketing agency for hire.** Agency State builds with the team (the brand source, automations and agents, checks and review) and works with its Brand OS lead as the work grows. It does not run the brand's marketing in the team's place.
- **Not enterprise consulting.** The work fits a single marketing function with a clear owner, not a multi-thousand-person transformation with dedicated AI teams and complex procurement.
- **Not a tutorial or course.** The work builds workflows and artifacts with the team. The work uses Claude Code and Codex.
- **Not a single-discipline specialty.** Greg is a generalist with the tools to execute across disciplines. SEO, paid-media, and analytics specialists exist; he is not one of them.

## What is Brand OS?

*Agency State’s Brand OS connects an approved brand source with marketing production, checks, and people who review work and approve updates.*

### What is Brand OS?, Why it matters

Leaders in the [2026 CMO Survey](https://cmosurvey-new.fuqua.duke.edu/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/The_CMO_Survey-Highlights_and_Insights_Report-2026.pdf) say they use AI in about a quarter of their marketing today and expect it in more than half within three years. Agencies and brand teams still answer for what ships. I build Brand OS with teams to give their AI work a brand source, checks against it, and people who review every draft. With it, the team has a basis for deciding whether the work represents its brand and is ready to ship.

A Brand OS has five parts. The codified brand comes first; the other four put it to work and keep it current.

```text
Brand OS:
  source:      the codified brand
  production:  automations and agents that make the work
  control:     checks against the source and the brand's rules
  feedback:    approved updates to source, checks, and review paths
  operators:   people who make the judgment calls and keep the source current
```

### What is Brand OS?, 01. Source

The source brings the team’s brand decisions together as shared guidance for production and review. It records who the brand is for, what it can claim, and how it looks and sounds, with the facts and evidence behind those decisions. Each entry records where it came from and whether it was stated or inferred. Interpretations and unresolved questions go to the team for review.

For a product launch, the source records the team's decisions about the facts and rules used in the product page, sales email, paid search copy, social posts, and internal review notes.

```text
launch source:
  product facts:     specifications, availability, names, compatibility
  claims:            approved language, evidence, required conditions
  voice:             vocabulary, banned words, channel differences
  visual:            color, type, logo, layout, image rules
  provenance:        source, stated or inferred, confidence, reviewer, date
  open questions:    gaps, conflicts, inferred items, decisions needed
```

Each automation or agent is served the parts of the source its task needs through MCP, an open standard for connecting AI tools to outside data. For agencies serving multiple clients, each client brand has its own source.

### What is Brand OS?, 02. Production

Production is where the team's work gets made. The team supplies the brief and the idea; the source supplies the brand guidance for the task. The work covers campaigns and the ongoing production between them: product updates, page revisions, email versions, social posts.

Agents draft, adapt, localize, and update that material. An email and a social post call for different choices about length, emphasis, and detail, and the agent makes those choices within the brief. Automations handle the set steps around each draft. They run the checks and send the draft to the person who reviews it. The reviewer receives the draft with the check results and any limits the checks report.

```text
production flow:
  from the team:     brief, idea, audience, deadline
  from the source:   facts, approved claims, voice, visual rules
  agents make:       product page, paid search, email, social, partner copy
  automations:       run the checks, send each draft for review
  to a person:       a review brief with the choices to make
```

### What is Brand OS?, 03. Control

Control separates what a machine can check from what a person should decide. Some questions have a right answer: whether a banned word appears, whether a claim includes its required condition, whether a product is in the catalog, and whether a color is in the approved palette.

A check answers the question it was built to ask, and passing it establishes only what it tested. In [a test I ran with Yowie](context-is-not-control.html), a demo outdoor-gear brand, Claude was asked for a cooler Yowie doesn't make and wrote a full product page for it. The page passed every rule check; only a check against the product list caught it. In the same test, a customer reply promised free repair or replacement, with shipping both ways. Yowie's brand source says nothing about warranties, so no check in that test could catch those promises.

```text
check result (Yowie demo-brand test):
  draft:          product page for the Slab 45QT cooler
  rule checks:    passed (banned words, punctuation, claim conditions)
  source check:   flagged; Slab 45QT is not in the catalog
```

Voice needs human review. In a recent study of three Claude models and one brand, the models given the brand's voice to imitate still wrote in their own voice.

> “Instructed to imitate a brand voice, the models remain almost perfectly separable from the brand's held-out text on style-only instruments. Asked to predict their own style, every model missed more often than it hit.”

Victor A. Barger, [“Does AI Have a Voice?”](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=7248819) working paper, 2026 (tested on three Claude models and one brand)

Other questions have no right answer. A claim may be technically allowed but weak. The voice may fit the rules and still sound wrong for the campaign. An image may follow the guidelines and still miss the brand. The checks run first. A person on the team reviews every draft, judges whether it looks and sounds like the brand and whether an allowed claim is worth making, and catches anything the checks missed. Decision owners get the questions the reviewer can't settle.

```text
for the reviewer:
  is this claim worth making?
  does the voice fit this campaign?
  does the image fit the brand?
  what have the checks missed?
```

### What is Brand OS?, 04. Feedback

A correction to a draft shouldn't automatically become a change to the brand. The problem may be an error in the draft, a case that calls for an exception, stale source material, or a rule that's too loose, too strict, or missing.

Each correction is recorded with what was decided and why. If that decision calls for a change to the source, checks, or review process, the owner of that decision approves the change before it’s applied. Accepted changes go back into the source, checks, or review process, and the next draft uses the change. We use approved updates to improve how the system represents the brand and keep it current as the brand changes.

```text
feedback outcomes:
  draft correction:     fix this draft
  approved exception:   record who approved the exception and why
  source update:        change the fact or approved language
  rule update:          add or revise a check
  routing update:       send this question to the right owner
```

### What is Brand OS?, 05. Operators

People make the brand decisions. They decide which exceptions are allowed, which rules should be enforced, and when source material needs updating.

Roles like the brand owner, product owner, claims owner, design owner, and Brand OS lead each take the decisions in their area. On a small team, one person may hold several roles. A reviewer sees every draft, and decision owners settle the questions the reviewer can't answer. Decision owners also keep the source current as changes are approved.

```text
operators (example roles):
  brand owner:       voice, positioning, boundaries
  product owner:     facts, specifications, availability
  claims owner:      approved claims, evidence, conditions
  design owner:      visual rules and exceptions
  Brand OS lead:     adoption, team communications, keeping the source current
```

### What is Brand OS?, How I work with a team

I start with a brand-readiness assessment, looking at the brand as it’s written down and as it shows up in the work. The brand itself stays with the team and its agencies. I organize the material with them and bring disagreements, interpretations, and gaps to the people who decide them. We review the structured source before it is approved for use by the team’s AI tools.

I can also help set up how the team works in Claude Code and Codex, organizing project files and repositories and configuring how agents receive brand guidance and project instructions.

Once the source is approved and being served, I work with the team to choose and build the first production automations and agents, agree on how every draft is reviewed, and decide how success will be measured. From there I work closely with the team, especially its Brand OS lead, tracking where the AI work misses the brand, making approved updates to the source and checks, and extending the system to more of the team’s work.

To request a brand-readiness assessment, send me an email.

## Context is not control

*Brand documents shaped how Claude's copy sounded. They didn't stop it from inventing a cooler.*

### Context is not control, The problem

Giving AI your written brand documents can help it write better marketing copy. Those documents become its context: the vocabulary, examples of the voice, product information, and the rules you want it to follow.

But a draft it writes can sound right while getting important things wrong. It can name a product you don't sell or promise a policy you don't offer. I've found these errors are different from the ones people make. They fit. They borrow real details, or they sound like what any brand like yours would say. The clues that they're wrong are there, but only someone who knows the facts will see them.

### Context is not control, The test

In a test with Yowie, a demo outdoor-gear brand, I gave Claude Opus 5 the brand information and product catalog, then sent it marketing requests that included misleading product details.

I ran two kinds of automated checks on every draft. Rule checks looked for banned words, exclamation points, and missing conditions on ice-retention claims. A source check found the model names in each draft, such as Slab 35QT, and looked them up in the catalog.

Thirty-six of the requests asked for a product that wasn't in the catalog. In 33 of them, Claude corrected the request, chose a real product, or avoided naming one. That was the right response.

But in three drafts, Claude went along with the request. One was a full product page for the Slab 45QT, a cooler that wasn't in Yowie's catalog. Claude invented a weight, dimensions, and a price.

    check result:
      draft:          product page for the Slab 45QT cooler
      rule checks:    passed
      source check:   flagged; Slab 45QT is not in the catalog

The catalog had a Slab 35QT, and Claude built the fake cooler out of it. The real 35QT weighs 22 lb and costs $285. The invented 45QT weighs 28 lb and costs $335. The page also reused the 35QT's ice-retention claim, 5+ days at 90°F, and because that claim named its test conditions, the page passed the rule checks. The fake was convincing because it was built partly from Yowie's real product information. It did leave a clue. The page called the 45QT "the larger of the two Slab sizes," though Yowie makes a single size. You'd catch that only if you knew the catalog.

All three fake drafts passed the rule checks, and the source check flagged all three. The rule checks asked whether the copy followed the brand's rules, and it did. The source check asked whether the product existed, and it didn't. This was one test on one brand with a short catalog, and a missing product is one of the easier errors for a check to spot.

The brand information gave Claude context, and that context did most of the work. Claude got 33 of the 36 right, and the three it got wrong looked just as finished as the rest. The brand information shaped how the page sounded, but it didn't control everything the page claimed. To know what's been checked, you have to know what each check asked.

### Context is not control, What a check can tell you

Some brand requirements are straightforward to check, such as whether a banned word appears or a color matches an approved value. If an approved claim requires an exact disclosure, a check can confirm it's there.

Other errors have nothing to be checked against. One request asked for a reply about warranty options to a customer with a snagging zipper on a Burrow 20F sleeping bag. Claude correctly said Yowie doesn't make a Burrow 20F, and the source check flagged that name. But the reply also promised a free repair or replacement, shipping both ways, and a two-to-three-week turnaround. Yowie's brand information has no warranty terms, so none of the checks in this test could catch those promises.

Finding a product in the catalog doesn't mean the draft describes its performance accurately. That requires comparing the draft's claims with the relevant product specifications. And for a claim about how long a cooler keeps ice, that means checking both the stated duration and the conditions used to test the product.

Some decisions need a person's judgment even when the facts are correct. A campaign can follow the vocabulary rules and still feel wrong. An approved claim can be a poor choice for a particular audience. A legal exception needs someone authorized to approve it.

### Context is not control, Where the corrections go

A person on the team reviews each draft. Corrections to that draft can reveal problems in the brand information the AI is working from.

If an old product fact keeps appearing, check whether the brand information still contains it. If the team keeps making the same edit, decide where the fix belongs. A phrase that should never appear belongs in the rules. A preference for how the copy should sound belongs in the voice guidance, supported by examples. Exact wording the team wants reused belongs in approved material. A mistake that should be caught on every draft belongs in a check.

If the team keeps approving an exception to a rule, someone needs to decide when that exception is allowed and record the decision. The team approves changes to the source, checks, or review process before future work uses them.

The brand information gives AI context. Control comes from the checks around it and the people who make the calls the checks can’t. With Agency State’s Brand OS, I build those checks and human review into the team’s automations and agents, using the same approved brand source for production and checking.

## Serving your brand to AI tools

*How MCP serves each AI tool the part of the brand it needs.*

### Serving your brand to AI tools, The job

MCP is an open standard for connecting AI tools to outside data. For a brand, MCP serves marketing tools the brand material they need for the task, from voice guidance to the full platform.

    serving layer:
      source stays governed
      tools ask for the right slice
      responses stay scoped to the task
      checks use the same source drafts use
      updates happen in one place

### Serving your brand to AI tools, Source and server

The source and the server are different things.

The source is approved brand material, including structured files, provenance, rules, decisions, and review notes. The server reads it and answers requests from tools.

The team governs the brand material. The server handles access, caching, tool names, response formats, and connections.

    pattern:
      brand source:
        maintained by the team
        reviewed and versioned
        structured for drafting and checks
    
      MCP server:
        reads the source
        exposes useful tools
        returns scoped answers
        connects to agents and workflows

### Serving your brand to AI tools, What tools ask for

Writing, review, and page builds each call for different brand material. A workflow handling an exception can request the decision owner.

Connecting a server makes its tools available, but an agent still needs instructions to request the right part. The workflow says what to request and when.

    requests by task:
      writing:     voice, never-say list
      review:      claims, proof, product facts
      page build:  visual rules
      exception:   the decision owner

Brand OS

I’ve built Agency State’s Brand OS to connect brand guidance with the marketing work a team makes and reviews. It gives the team a shared process it can manage and extend as its use of AI grows.

I prepare the brand for use with AI, drawing on its existing written and visual material to build a source that records where the guidance came from. I bring gaps, interpretations, and conflicts to the team to decide. Once approved, the source supplies AI tools with the brand guidance they need for each task.

I can also help set up how the team works in Claude Code and Codex, organizing project files and repositories and configuring how agents receive brand guidance and project instructions. The setup gives people a consistent way to work together and maintain what they build.

I build automations and agents for work such as preparing briefs, updating product pages, or adapting campaign material for different channels. Automated checks use the same approved source to flag potential errors. Each draft goes to a person on the team with the check results, any limits the checks report, and questions that need human judgment, such as whether the work emphasizes the right benefits, fits the campaign, and sounds like the brand.

A correction can fix one draft or reveal a need to update the source, the checks, or the review process. The team approves those changes before future work uses them, so an edit to one draft does not automatically become a rule for later work. We use approved updates to improve how the system represents the brand and keep it current as the brand changes.
